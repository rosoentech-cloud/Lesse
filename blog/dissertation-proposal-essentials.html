What a Dissertation Proposal Needs to Include

The core sections of a dissertation or thesis proposal, including background, research question, method, timeline, and expected contribution, with guidance for each.

Published 2026-11-12

A dissertation proposal persuades your committee that your project is worth doing and that you can complete it. Requirements vary by university, but most proposals share the same core sections.

Core sections

1. Introduction and background. Explain the problem, the context, and why it matters. Keep it focused on the gap your study addresses. 2. Literature review. Summarize the most important work and show the gap. The review in a proposal is shorter than the one in a final thesis, but the logic must be the same. 3. Research questions or hypotheses. State them precisely. Each question should suggest a method for answering it. 4. Methodology. Describe your design, participants or data, procedures, and how you will analyze results. Justify each choice. 5. Timeline. Break the project into stages with realistic dates. 6. Expected contribution. Explain what your findings will add to the field and who might use them. 7. References. List every source you cite, in your department's style.

Common weaknesses

  • A research question that cannot be answered with the proposed method
  • A methodology section that describes procedures without justifying them
  • A timeline that ignores ethics approval or data collection delays
  • A literature review that lists sources without showing how they relate

Before you submit

Ask your supervisor to read a near-final draft. Check the department's guidelines for length, structure, and formatting, and confirm whether ethics or institutional review is required before data collection begins.

A proposal is a plan. It does not need to predict every result, but it does need to show that you have thought through how to get them.
