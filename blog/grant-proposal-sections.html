Grant Proposal Sections Funders Look For

The sections most grant proposals require, including the statement of need, objectives, methods, evaluation, and budget narrative, with tips for matching a funder's criteria.

Published 2026-11-07

A grant proposal is a structured argument for funding. Reviewers often read dozens of applications, so clear sections that match their criteria matter as much as the idea itself. Read the funder's guidelines before you write anything.

Common sections

  • Statement of need. Describe the problem, who it affects, and the evidence that it exists. Use local data where you have it.
  • Goals and objectives. Goals are broad aims. Objectives are measurable steps toward them, with timeframes.
  • Methods or activities. Explain what you will do, who will do it, and why this approach fits the need.
  • Evaluation plan. State how you will measure progress and what success looks like.
  • Sustainability. Describe how the work will continue after the grant ends.
  • Budget and budget narrative. Explain each budget line, and make sure the numbers match the activities in the text.

Match the funder's language

Reviewers score proposals against criteria, often in a rubric. Use the funder's terms for their priorities, and address each criterion directly. A reviewer should be able to find where you meet each one.

Keep claims supportable

Do not promise outcomes you cannot back up. Use realistic targets, state your assumptions, and cite sources for statistics. An honest plan with a modest goal is more credible than an ambitious promise with no basis.

Before you submit

Check the page limits, font and margin rules, required attachments, and the deadline in the funder's time zone. Ask a colleague who did not write the proposal to read it. Their questions reveal the gaps you have stopped noticing.
