How to Write a Strong Introduction Paragraph

A simple structure for opening paragraphs in essays and reports that hooks the reader, establishes context, and leads clearly into your thesis.

Published 2026-11-04

Your introduction has one job: to convince the reader that your question matters and to show where your argument is going. Many introductions drift into general statements that could open any paper. A focused structure avoids that problem.

A useful structure

1. Open with context. Start with the subject at a level specific enough to be interesting. Avoid sweeping statements such as "Since the beginning of time, humans have..." 2. Narrow to the problem. Explain what is known, what is contested, or what is missing from the existing discussion. 3. State your thesis. Give your claim in one or two sentences. 4. Preview the structure. Briefly indicate how the essay will support the claim.

Example

Weak opening: "Technology is everywhere in modern life, and it has changed how people learn."

Stronger opening: "Since schools began issuing tablets in 2015, reading assessments have shown mixed results. Some studies report gains in engagement, while others find weaker comprehension. This essay argues that the outcome depends less on the device than on how teachers integrate it into reading lessons."

The stronger version moves from context, to the problem, to a clear thesis within three sentences.

Common problems

  • A dictionary definition opening. Readers have already seen it. Define only the term your argument depends on.
  • Announcing instead of arguing. "In this essay, I will discuss..." adds nothing. Show the argument itself.
  • A thesis that appears too late. If the reader must read two pages before finding your claim, move it up.

Length

Most essay introductions are one paragraph. Long reports may need two. Keep the introduction short enough that the reader reaches your thesis quickly.
