Results vs Discussion: Keeping Them Separate

Why the results section should report findings without interpretation, and how to write a discussion section that explains what the findings mean in context.

Published 2026-11-16

Many research papers blur results and discussion, which makes both harder to evaluate. The results section reports what you found. The discussion explains what it means. Keeping them apart lets readers check your evidence before they judge your interpretation.

Results: report, do not interpret

Present findings in the order of your research questions. Use text, tables, and figures together, and describe each one in words that a reader can follow without the figure. Report the key statistics, such as sample size, effect size, and confidence intervals where relevant.

Avoid explaining why a result happened in this section. "Scores increased by 12 percent" is a result. "Scores increased because students enjoyed the activity" is an interpretation.

Discussion: interpret carefully

The discussion should:

  • Start by restating the main findings in plain language.
  • Compare them with the literature in your review, noting agreements and disagreements.
  • Explain possible reasons for unexpected results.
  • Acknowledge limitations that affect how the findings should be read.
  • State what the findings mean for theory, practice, or future research.

Common mistakes

  • Introducing new results in the discussion
  • Claiming causation from correlational data
  • Ignoring findings that do not support your argument
  • Repeating the results section word for word

A quick check

Ask yourself, for each sentence in the results section, whether it describes evidence or explains meaning. Move any explanation into the discussion. Then check that every claim in the discussion points back to a result a reader can find.
