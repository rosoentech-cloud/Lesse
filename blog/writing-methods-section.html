How to Write a Methods Section People Can Follow

A guide to writing a clear methods section that lets readers understand, evaluate, and repeat your study, with a checklist for the details to include.

Published 2026-11-14

The methods section explains exactly what you did, so that a reader can judge whether your findings are credible and could repeat the study. Vague methods make even strong results hard to trust.

What to include

  • Design. Whether the study was experimental, observational, qualitative, or a review, and why that design fits the question.
  • Participants or data. Who or what was studied, how they were selected, and how many there were.
  • Materials and instruments. Surveys, tests, equipment, or software, with versions where relevant.
  • Procedure. The steps in the order they happened, written in past tense.
  • Analysis. The statistical or analytical methods used, and the reason for each.
  • Ethics. Approval numbers or consent procedures where required.

Write for replication

Ask yourself whether a competent researcher could repeat the study from your description alone. If they would need to contact you for a missing detail, add it.

Keep it factual

The methods section reports what happened. Save interpretation for the discussion. Avoid phrases that judge the design, such as "the excellent sample," unless you explain the criteria behind that description.

Common problems

  • Omitting how participants were recruited or excluded
  • Naming a software package without its version
  • Describing a procedure you planned but did not follow, without noting the change
  • Burying a deviation from the protocol instead of reporting it openly

Check against your protocol

Compare your section with the ethics application or study plan. If you changed anything during the study, describe the change and why you made it. Honest reporting of deviations strengthens your work.
