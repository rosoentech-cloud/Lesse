How to Give Useful Peer Feedback on a Draft

A practical guide to giving constructive feedback on classmates' essays and drafts, covering what to look for, how to phrase comments, and how to prioritize.

Published 2026-10-30

Peer review helps writers see gaps they cannot see in their own drafts, and it helps reviewers sharpen their own judgment. Good feedback is specific, prioritized, and kind without being vague. It respects the writer's ideas while being honest about what is not working.

Read twice

On the first read, follow the argument without marking anything. Note your overall response: what is the paper claiming, and do you find it convincing? On the second read, leave comments tied to specific sentences or paragraphs.

Prioritize

Start with the largest issues:

1. Is the thesis clear? 2. Does the evidence support the argument? 3. Is the organization logical? 4. Are the sentences clear? 5. Are there errors in citations or grammar?

Do not spend your comments on commas if the argument has no clear claim. Writers can only address a few changes at once.

Phrase comments usefully

  • Describe what you noticed, then explain the effect. "I lost track of the argument here, because the example comes before the claim."
  • Ask questions that help the writer think. "What evidence would convince a skeptical reader of this point?"
  • Point out strengths too. Writers need to know what to keep.

Avoid comments such as "this is wrong" or "good job" with no detail. Neither helps the writer improve.

Respect ownership

Feedback suggests; the writer decides. If you think a sentence should change, explain the reason rather than rewriting it for the writer. Rewriting a whole paragraph is not feedback, and it can cross academic integrity rules in many courses.

Finish with a summary

End your review with three to five sentences: the paper's main strength, its most important problem, and what you would revise first.
