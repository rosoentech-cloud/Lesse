An academic CV is longer than a resume and organized around research. It can run to several pages, and it is expected to list every publication, presentation, and grant. Readers use it to judge your research record, teaching experience, and fit with their group.

## Common sections

- **Contact details and current position**
- **Education**, including thesis titles and supervisors
- **Research experience** and research interests
- **Publications**, split into peer-reviewed articles, book chapters, and other work
- **Presentations and conference talks**
- **Grants, fellowships, and awards**
- **Teaching experience** and courses taught
- **Service**, such as reviewing for journals or organizing events
- **References**, or a statement that they are available on request

## Format publications consistently

Use one citation style for the whole list, and put the most recent items first. Mark the status of each item clearly, such as "in press" or "under review." Do not list a manuscript as published until it is.

## Keep the language factual

State what you did and what it produced. Avoid unsupported adjectives such as "groundbreaking." Let your record speak for itself.

## Tailor it to each application

Adjust the research summary and the order of sections for each position. A teaching-focused post may need your teaching section first, while a research post may need your publications first.

## Check the requirements

Some funders and departments specify a page limit or a format. Check each call. Keep a master version of your CV with everything, and create shorter versions from it.
