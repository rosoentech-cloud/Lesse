A thesis statement tells your reader what your paper argues and why it matters. It usually sits at the end of your introduction, and everything else in the paper should support it.

## What a thesis is not

A thesis is not a topic. "Climate change" is a topic. "Climate change affects the world" is a fact most readers already accept. A useful thesis takes a position that someone could reasonably disagree with.

## A simple test

Ask yourself whether your statement passes these three checks:

- **It makes a claim.** Your reader should be able to say whether they agree.
- **It is specific.** It names the group, period, place, or mechanism you are talking about.
- **It previews the argument.** A reader should be able to guess the structure of your paper from it.

## Example

Weak: "Social media has advantages and disadvantages for teenagers."

Stronger: "Although social media gives teenagers access to support communities, its design rewards constant comparison, which makes it harmful for adolescents with low self-esteem."

The stronger version commits to a position, names who it is about, and hints at how the paper will proceed.

## Revise it at the end

Your argument often changes as you write. Once your draft is finished, reread the body paragraphs and check that they support the thesis as it now stands. If they do not, change the thesis to match what you actually argued.

- Write a first version to get started.
- Test it against the three checks above.
- Revise it after you finish the draft.
