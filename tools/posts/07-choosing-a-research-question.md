A good research question tells you what to search for, what method to use, and what counts as an answer. A vague question leads to vague research. The goal is a question narrow enough to finish and wide enough to matter.

## Start with a topic you care about

Interest keeps you going through months of reading. List three topics you would enjoy reading about, then narrow each one to a specific population, place, period, or mechanism.

## Test your question

Check your draft question against these points:

- **Is it specific?** Name the group, setting, or time frame.
- **Is it answerable?** You should be able to find evidence, collect data, or make an argument within your deadline.
- **Is it open?** It should not have an obvious answer that everyone already accepts.
- **Does it matter?** A reader should understand why the answer would be useful.

## Compare two versions

Too broad: "How does technology affect education?"

Workable: "How do secondary school teachers in one district report using tablets for reading instruction, and what barriers do they describe?"

The second version names the population, the tool, the focus, and the kind of evidence you would need.

## Check the literature early

Spend a short session searching your database before you commit. If a question has already been answered many times, look for a more specific angle. If nothing has been written on it, check whether your question is too narrow or too unusual to be researchable.

## Talk to your supervisor or instructor

Bring two or three candidate questions to a meeting. Ask which is feasible in your timeframe. Their feedback will save you weeks of work on a question that cannot be finished.
