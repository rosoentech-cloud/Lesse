Paraphrasing means restating a source's idea in your own words and sentence structure, then citing it. Changing a few words in a sentence is not enough. Your reader needs to see that you understood the idea and rewrote it.

## Why swapping synonyms fails

Replacing words with synonyms while keeping the original sentence order is still copying. If your paraphrase follows the source line by line, you are reproducing its structure, which counts as plagiarism even when the words are different.

## A method that works

1. **Read the passage until you understand it.** Close the source and explain the idea in one sentence, as if to a friend.
2. **Write from memory.** Draft your version without looking at the original.
3. **Check against the source.** Make sure the meaning is accurate and that you have not copied any phrase of five or more words without quotation marks.
4. **Cite it.** Add the author and year (or page) for the idea, even though the wording is yours.

## Example

Original idea: a study found that students who wrote summaries by hand remembered more than those who typed them.

Weak paraphrase: "A study showed that students who wrote summaries by hand retained more than students who typed them." (Too close to the original wording and structure.)

Stronger paraphrase: "Handwritten summaries may improve memory more than typed ones, according to one experimental study (Chen, 2019)."

## When to quote instead

Quote directly when the exact wording matters, such as a definition or a memorable phrase. Keep quotations short, and always put them in quotation marks with a page number.

## Final check

Ask yourself: could a reader tell which words came from the source without the citation? If the answer is yes, rewrite the sentence.
