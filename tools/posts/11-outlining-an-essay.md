An outline turns your research into a plan. It shows what each paragraph will do, which evidence supports it, and how the argument moves from one point to the next. Thirty focused minutes on an outline can save hours of rewriting.

## Step 1: Write your claim in one sentence

Use your thesis statement, or write a working version. Everything in the outline must serve this claim.

## Step 2: List your main points

Write down three to five points that support the claim. Each one should be a complete sentence, not a topic. "Remote work reduces commuting costs" is a point. "Commuting" is not.

## Step 3: Attach evidence

Under each point, list the sources, data, or examples you will use. If a point has no evidence, either find some or cut the point.

## Step 4: Order the points

Arrange the points so that each one builds on the last. Ask: does the reader need to understand point A before point B makes sense? If so, A comes first.

## Step 5: Plan the counterargument

Strong essays address objections. Add one paragraph that states the strongest opposing view and explains why your claim still holds.

## Sample outline

- **Introduction:** context, thesis
- **Point 1:** commuting costs (evidence: two cost surveys)
- **Point 2:** productivity measures (evidence: a field experiment)
- **Counterargument:** collaboration suffers when teams are remote
- **Conclusion:** what the evidence means for employers

## Keep it flexible

Your outline is a map, not a contract. Revise it when your reading changes your view. Updating the outline before you write is much cheaper than rebuilding a finished draft.
