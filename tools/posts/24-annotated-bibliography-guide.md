An annotated bibliography is a list of sources, each followed by a short paragraph called an annotation. The annotation summarizes the source and evaluates it. Instructors assign annotated bibliographies to check that students have read carefully and can judge what they have read.

## What each annotation includes

A typical annotation has two parts:

1. **Summary.** The source's main argument, question, method, and findings, in your own words. Usually two to four sentences.
2. **Evaluation.** Your assessment of the source's relevance, reliability, and usefulness for your project. Explain why you would or would not rely on it.

## Example structure

Citation in your chosen style, followed by:

- A summary sentence on the research question and method
- A sentence on the main finding
- A sentence on the source's limitations or strengths
- A sentence on how it will help your own paper

## Writing good evaluations

Avoid general praise such as "This is an excellent source." Instead, say what makes it useful. For example, it uses a large national sample, it compares two methods directly, or it is older than most of the field's recent work and may be outdated.

## Keep the list consistent

- Use the same citation style throughout.
- Alphabetize entries by author.
- Keep annotations to a similar length unless the assignment says otherwise.

## Why it helps your research

Writing annotations while you read turns passive reading into active notes. Those notes become the raw material for your literature review and your thesis, which is why many students find the assignment more useful than they expected.
